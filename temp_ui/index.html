Why flames behave differently in orbit

On Earth, buoyancy lifts hot gas away from a flame and draws fresh air in. In free-fall that flow disappears, so flames can be dimmer, rounder and able to survive in conditions where an Earth test says they would go out. Spacecraft fire research, such as NASA's Saffire experiments, studies exactly this.

This tool trains a neural network in your browser on microgravity-style combustion records and turns it into practical answers: will a flame persist, how fast will it spread, and what would reduce the risk?

Data notice. This build uses a physics-informed synthetic dataset that mimics the structure of spacecraft combustion experiments. It is a demonstration, not a certified safety tool, and it is an independent project, not an official NASA product. Load real experiment data in the Data tab before drawing conclusions.

What the AI does

  1. Generate data. Thousands of virtual experiments are simulated from a reduced-order physics model with measurement noise.
  2. Train two neural networks. One predicts whether the flame persists; the other predicts its spread rate.
  3. Ask what-if questions. The insights engine re-runs your scenario at other gravity levels, oxygen levels, airflows and pressures to explain the risk.
